AI Champions Program Field Guide

Eight stages in two parts. First, set up the program. Then, help each champion take real use cases from idea to value.

For AI leaders who are starting a champions network, or fixing one that has lost momentum.

The problem

Wins stay local

Useful AI discoveries often reach only one or two colleagues. Better tool knowledge alone does not make them spread.

The fix

A trusted peer, one step ahead

A champion sits inside the team’s real work, formally named or already doing it informally, and makes local wins into shared practice.

The skill

Influence, not authority

Strong tool use is not the same as teaching. Champions show everyday examples and methods others can reuse. This holds for small teams too.

I

Program setup

Create the conditions. The AI leader and sponsors own these four stages. Without them, strong individuals cannot become a working network.

Part I · stage 1 of 8

Decide what the network is for and how far champions may go. Then write it down with IT, security, and the other decision-makers.

Same task, different owner. A team spends hours each week on a customer-renewal summary. All three roles can take it on. What changes is who builds and who gets the handoff. So do not train or judge every champion like a full-time engineer.

Write the charter: answer six questions
1

PurposeWhat result? A use-case funnel to a central team, broad adoption, local build capacity, or a mix. Structure and training follow this choice.

2

Build rightsWho may build for themselves, for others, or only use? Make this a deliberate choice, not an accident of tool access.

3

Everyone elseWhat do you expect from people who are not champions? The program changes their job too.

4

SpreadHow will what is built reach people in the field? Champions and tools alone are not a mechanism.

5

Central teams & vendorsHow do champions work with tool owners, IT, security, specialist builders, and suppliers? Decide this even with no central AI team.

6

IncentivesDo rewards and expectations match the company’s AI strategy?

Revisit the scope as you learn. The right level of decentralization changes over time.

2

Part I · stage 2 of 8

Choose the people

Pick people who already do the job informally, and whose manager will give them real time to do it.

✓Look for evidence

  • Uses AI well without being told to
  • Peers already ask them for help
  • Knows the business and its pain points
  • Initiative and curiosity
  • Influence without formal authority
  • Keeps learning as the field changes

!Require

  • Real capacity. The manager frees time to help others, not only for the champion’s own tasks. One or two days is the episode’s example, not a rule.
  • A career path. The title raises a person’s profile, inside and outside. Pick people you want to keep and promote.
  • A manager who knows how to use the champion.

×Avoid four traps

  • The senior self-nominee. Strategic leaders belong in steering, not peer building.
  • The brilliant but busy. Capacity is part of the fit, not a detail for later.
  • The reward nominee. Good general performance does not replace AI skill.
  • The brand-new hire. Without business context they cannot spot good use cases.
3

Part I · stage 3 of 8

Train on real work

Train the full role, not only the tools. Each champion proves readiness by moving one real team use case forward.

How to train

  • Any format works: internal sessions, a bootcamp, or gradual learning. Anchor it on the team’s own work. Even excellent users need preparation.
  • Readiness test: by the end, each person has advanced or built one real use case. No progress means more preparation.
  • Mark the start: a kickoff or certification creates energy. The real test is the day after.

What to teach

  • Judgment across business, technology, and people: use-case selection, evaluation, adoption, and handoff.
  • Not only experimentation. Trying tools teaches tool use. It rarely teaches how to evaluate.
  • Why invest here: training a small group spreads locally relevant skill further. This is the hosts’ experience, not a measured ROI.
4

Part I · stage 4 of 8

Keep it alive

Give the network two sponsors, a steady rhythm, and an easy way in and out. Name an owner for what happens after launch.

The sponsor pair: two functions, not two job titles
Community sponsorOften HR, L&D, or People. Keeps participation, learning, and recognition alive.
AI sponsorLinks to the chief AI officer or equal. Sets priorities, clears access and approvals, bridges to IT and security.
↓ stay close to what champions need and build ↓
Embedded championsTrusted colleagues who know the team’s work
↕ a working relationship both ways ↕
ManagersProtect time; know how to use the role
PeersBring problems; use and share results
Central teamsTools, security, specialist builds

The strongest example in the episode: an L&D expert and an AI expert working as a pair.

The rhythm after launch

  • Regular meetings and an easy way to share work
  • Learning together, including new tools and methods
  • Celebrate outcomes that match the purpose, not activity
  • A short manager briefing: the role, the expected output, how to use it. Mismatched expectations frustrate both sides.
  • Normal entry, exit, and handover when the role no longer fits
  • Continuous education, not a one-time course
  • A named owner for continuation. Without follow-through, people go back to old ways of working.

Restarting a tired network? Fix the conditions before you plan another training event. Restore both sponsors, check real capacity with managers, renew commitments, let people leave with respect, and bring new people in.

II

Use case delivery

Turn real work into used, trusted solutions. Each champion owns these four stages, or hands them off according to the charter. They need not do every step, but they must understand the whole path: before the build, proof of concept to production, deployment, value, and retirement.

5

Part II · stage 5 of 8

Find worthwhile work

Start with a real, recurring problem, not a tool. Know what the company permits before you plan anything.

Opportunity brieffill before any build
Who has the problem?Name the people, not “everyone.”
How often?Recurring work earns investment. One-offs rarely do.
What does done look like?The end state, in the team’s words.
What does it cost today?Time, effort, quality. This is your baseline.
Which KPI does it move?Link it to a real company performance indicator.
Does it already exist?Check before you build a copy somewhere else.
Is it permitted?Tools, data, and actions the company allows.

Be conservative

Enthusiasm is not a business case.

  • Assume the cost will be higher than your first estimate.
  • Assume early value will be lower than you hope.
  • State expected benefit against cost, and how people will actually use it.
  • Say what evidence will show the goal was met.
6

Part II · stage 6 of 8

Choose the response

Building is only one option. If you do build, pick the simplest form that works.

First: should it exist?

Is this worth doing at all?

  • Is it a concrete, recurring problem?
  • Is the value worth the investment?
  • What would a mistake cost, and can the work tolerate errors?
  • Does something usable already exist?
ReuseBuyBuildDefer

Choose for the company’s needs, not because the build is exciting. Watch for a bias toward building.

Then: match the form to the need
  1. 1
    Shared chat setup

    Reusable instructions, prompts, and files. Often enough.

  2. 2
    Skill or playbook

    A repeatable method people invoke when needed.

  3. 3
    Shared tool or artifact

    A small app, page, or custom assistant others open and use. It needs an owner who keeps it current.

  4. 4
    Automation

    Fixed steps that run the same way each time.

  5. 5
    Agent

    Only when the need justifies it. Never the default.

Not a maturity ladder · + GUM-AI additionbars = build, upkeep, testing, risk

Each step up adds more to build, maintain, and test. Require a reason before you climb.

7

Part II · stage 7 of 8

Make it testable

Write the spec before the build. It becomes the handoff for a vendor, a central team, or the champion. Let the tests stop a weak idea early.

1

A specific user

A solution for everyone has no useful design target.

2

The knowledge it needs

Check the material exists and is usable. Curation can be a project of its own.

3

A real path into use

Design for how people will find and use it, not only for a finished app.

4

What it must never do

List prohibited actions in detail. Starting here often reveals hidden requirements.

5

Where humans decide

Name who reviews outputs and which decisions stay with people.

6

Evidence that it works

Write test cases early, including hard edge cases. If you cannot reach enough quality, stop.

8

Part II · stage 8 of 8

Deploy & sustain

Value is real only when the right people use it, often enough, and trust it. Stay involved until that happens.

Put it into real work

  • Right people, right frequency, right workflow. Publishing a tool is not deployment.
  • Name the home, the approver, and the support for after the launch excitement fades.
  • Stay to the end. A handoff to specialists does not end the champion’s part. Return for deployment and adoption.

Judge it honestly

  • Know the trust limits: when outputs are good enough, when they are not, and what errors cost. Release judgment is a core skill.
  • Retire it when it cannot meet quality needs or no longer pays back.

What stays true as tools change

Keep tool training current. These needs do not go away when the tools improve.

The fundamentals hold

The right people with time, an explicit role scope, an understanding of the full lifecycle, and ownership after training.

Enable subject experts

Do not simply block nontraditional builders. They often see the best use cases. Give them training, infrastructure, scope, and guardrails.

More capable agents need more governance

As coding and agents reach across systems, plan for containment, access, security, quality, and where people must decide.

The role builds careers

Champions see the beginning and the end of delivery, not only the build. That makes the role a strong development path.
